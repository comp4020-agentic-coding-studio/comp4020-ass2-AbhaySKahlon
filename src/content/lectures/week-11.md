A café chalkboard reads: "Orders usually ready in 10 minutes." You can read
it, note it down, and move on — that much is right there in front of you.
What you can't do is ask the next customer what they actually expected
walking in, or whether that sign is what set it. Nothing about this week's
mechanism has changed since Week 3. What's changed is your position: you're
no longer the person in the queue, you're the person looking at one from
outside.

## This week's claim, plainly

*The four mechanisms your own log could report on directly become open,
sometimes unanswerable questions once you stand outside someone else's
wait; learning to say "undetermined" honestly is the skill this week
teaches.*

Weeks 1 to 4 built time, attention, expectation, and information entirely
out of your own log — you were both the observer and the person whose
experience was being recorded, so every field had a direct answer available
just by asking yourself. Weeks 5 to 9 taught five different mechanisms —
interface, environment, queue structure, priority, value — that are
externally observable by design: a queue's posted rule or a room's layout
doesn't require access to anyone's mind. This week is where those two
halves of the course meet a genuine problem: the Queue Case Study asked you
to classify the second five from outside a system, but it never asked you
to say how strongly your evidence actually supported any claim about
effect. And the first four have never been asked of an external system at
all. Both gaps close here, on the one system you already know.

## Two axes, kept apart

- **Mechanism status** — present / absent / ambiguous / cannot determine.
  This is the question your Queue Case Study already asked, for a
  different five mechanisms: *what does the accessible evidence indicate is
  actually in the system?*
- **Evidence tier** — directly evidenced / plausible interpretation /
  undetermined. This is a different question, asked for the first time this
  week: *however I've classified the mechanism, how strongly does what I
  actually have support the specific claim I want to make about it?*

These are not two names for the same judgement, and they never combine into
one score. A mechanism's status can be settled with confidence from a
single observable feature. A claim about that mechanism's *effect* on
anyone's experience usually can't be, because that would require exactly
the kind of access — asking the person directly, running a controlled test
— that looking at a system from outside doesn't give you.

## Reading absence and uncertainty correctly

The evidence tier attaches to the specific claim you're making, not to
whether the mechanism is present. Two priority findings, both from a
published policy, show how differently that can land:

**Example A.** "The published policy states no priority criterion applies
here." Mechanism status: **absent**. Evidence tier: **directly evidenced**
— the policy says so, in writing, and you can point to exactly where.

**Example B.** "No priority mechanism could be identified from what's
published." Mechanism status: **cannot determine**. Evidence tier:
**undetermined** — not because nothing was found, but because the available
evidence isn't sufficient to establish whether an unstated mechanism exists
at all. A silence in a published policy is not the same kind of evidence as
a policy that explicitly rules something out.

Both are legitimate, complete findings. Neither is a weaker answer than
"present," and neither should be written up as if it were a guess.

## The five you already have

Your Queue Case Study gave interface, environment, queue structure,
priority, and value each a status finding — present, absent, ambiguous, or
cannot determine — on your own system. It did not give any of them an
evidence tier, because that question hadn't been taught yet. This week
doesn't ask you to redo that analysis. It asks you to go back to each of
those five findings and add the second axis: for whatever claim you made
about what that mechanism does, how strongly does your original evidence
actually support it? A status finding you're confident in can still carry a
claim about effect that only earns "plausible interpretation" — that
combination is common and correct, not a sign your Case Study was weak.

## The four you're building from scratch

Time, attention, expectation, and information have never been classified on
an external system at all — only on your own log. Doing it now means
applying both axes, not just the one your Case Study used:

- **Time.** A posted schedule, or a duration you time yourself, gives you a
  directly observable clock duration. Whether anyone standing in that
  system judged it as longer or shorter than that number is not observable
  — you have no channel to their estimate.
- **Attention.** A screen, seating, a view, silence, or nothing at all is a
  directly observable feature of the setting. Whether it actually occupied
  anyone's attention, or moved anyone's judged duration the way Week 2's
  sources describe, stays at most a plausible interpretation.
- **Information.** A display, an announcement, or their absence is directly
  observable. Their effect on anyone's evaluation of the wait is a separate
  question this week doesn't try to settle — it's the same mechanism Week 4
  named, just no longer measured on yourself.
- **Expectation** — this week's fully worked case, below.

## Expectation, worked in full

Back to the chalkboard: "Orders usually ready in 10 minutes."

- **Expectation-setting feature: present.** The sign exists, says a number,
  and you can quote it exactly. Not in question.
- **A specific customer's actual expectation: cannot determine.** The sign
  tells you what the system invites people to expect. It tells you nothing
  about what any particular person walking up to the counter actually
  believed — that would need asking them, which this kind of observation
  doesn't permit.
- **Claim: "the sign shaped that customer's expectation": plausible
  interpretation, unless direct evidence exists.** It's a reasonable
  inference — posted estimates are a standard way of setting a reference
  point, and Week 3's Pruyn and Smidts material supports that kind of
  reference point mattering in general — but nothing here confirms it for
  any actual customer at this café. The sign being there is never, on its
  own, evidence that anyone's internal expectation was actually set by it.

## What Weeks 1 to 4's sources do and don't give you here

Block and Zakay's prospective/retrospective finding, Block, Hancock and
Zakay's cognitive-load finding, Pruyn and Smidts' acceptable-waiting-time
finding, and Hui and Tse's information finding were all built the same way:
someone asked the person doing the waiting, directly, under a controlled
protocol ([Block & Zakay, 1997](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03209393); [Block,
Hancock & Zakay, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2010.03.006); [Pruyn
& Smidts, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-8116(98)00008-1); [Hui & Tse,
1996](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299606000206)). That's exactly the
channel you don't have when you're reading a system from outside without
interviewing anyone in it. These four sources aren't being reapplied to
your café or your Case Study system as if their findings transferred
automatically — they're evidence for why "undetermined" is the honest
answer to a claim about effect, not evidence about your particular system
at all.

## Why this matters

Every mechanism from Week 1 onward has, until now, been measured either on
yourself or on a system whose structure doesn't require anyone's inner
state to read. This week is where those two kinds of evidence have to sit
side by side on the same system, honestly tiered. Control is not part of
this week's table at all — it's supplied separately by your Week 10
experiment, tiered according to how strong that specific design actually
was, and carried into the Audit on its own terms rather than folded into
the nine mechanisms here. By the end of this week you hold nine mechanisms,
each with a status and a tier; Week 12 adds the tenth and asks for the
whole system at once, under the same thesis this course opened with —
waiting is an experience, not merely a duration.

## What you're doing this week

The session has you build one working table: your five Case Study findings,
re-tiered: your four remaining mechanisms, classified on both axes for the
first time, on the same system. That table is not a rehearsal for the
Waiting Audit — it's the first nine rows of it.
