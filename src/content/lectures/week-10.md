Two queues, identical length, identical information at every point along
the way. In one, you're free to step out and rejoin whenever you like. In
the other, a ticket system means leaving forfeits your place, so once
you've joined, you're in until it's over. Nothing about the duration or
what you're told has changed. Something else has — and this week names
that something.

## This week's claim, plainly

*When a wait's clock duration, disclosed information, and structure stay
the same, one thing changes — whether the person waiting has any
meaningful choice over whether, when, or under what conditions they
remain in it — and that difference is what this course names control.*

**Control is the degree to which a waiting person has, or perceives
themselves to have, meaningful choice over whether, when, or under what
conditions they remain in the waiting situation.**

Set this immediately against Week 4's mechanism, because the two are
easy to blur:

- **Information:** "I know what is happening."
- **Control:** "I have an available choice or action that changes my
  situation."

A countdown or a queue-position display can be fully informative while
offering no choice at all. Control is not "having information," and it
is not generic empowerment, self-determination, or autonomy in the
broad psychological sense — it is specifically about a person's choice
over their own presence in a wait.

## Nine mechanisms this isn't

Time asks how long a wait was judged to be, not whether anyone had a say
in it — a high-control and a low-control wait can be judged equally
long. Attention is what occupies the mind, independent of agency: you
can be fully occupied with no way to leave, or bored with total freedom
to. Expectation is a belief fixed before a wait starts; control concerns
what's actually available once it's underway. Interfaces represent a
process; control is about what a person can *do*, not what's shown to
them. Environment is a physical or digital backdrop — and this needs
saying plainly, because this week's own source uses crowding as part of
its design: **the density of other people in a space is Week 6's
mechanism. This week's mechanism is the choice a person has over
entering or remaining in that setting, not the crowding itself.** Queue
structure is an external ordering rule, verifiable or not, independent
of any one person's agency within it. Priority is a class-based
criterion imposed from outside; a person can have no priority and full
control, or top priority and none. Value is what's exchanged for time —
a paid queue-skip can still lock someone into a slot they can't leave,
and a free queue can still be one they can abandon at will.

## What Hui and Bateson actually studied

Hui and Bateson study perceived control as a mediator of consumer
response within a service encounter ([Hui & Bateson,
1991](https://doi.org/10.1086/209250)). Their design examines two
conditions: consumer density — how crowded the setting is — and
consumer choice, meaning specifically whether entering into, and
staying in, the service situation was the person's own decision. Their
result: perceived control mediates the effect of both density and
choice on how pleasant the service experience is judged to be, and on
whether the response is approach or avoidance.

Read that at exactly the scope it earns. The choice variable they tested
is a "whether" question — whether entering and staying was voluntary at
all. This course's definition above widens that to "when" and "under
what conditions" a person can remain — a deliberate course-level
extension of the same idea, in the same way Week 7 named "legibility"
as this course's own term rather than one borrowed intact from a
source. Keep the two apart: the "whether" component is what Hui and
Bateson's design actually tests; "when" and "under what conditions" is
this course's own extension of it, not a further finding of theirs.

**This evidence concerns service encounters and perceived control, not
waiting duration itself.** Hui and Bateson did not measure judged
waiting duration. Their result does not establish that control makes a
wait feel shorter. Their setting is a service encounter in general, not
a physical queue specifically, and a single study of one context
doesn't generalise to waiting systems as a whole.

## What this doesn't establish

None of this licenses "giving people control shortens a wait" or "more
choice always improves an experience" as general claims. It licenses
something narrower: within a service encounter, perceived control was
shown to carry the effect of crowding and of a choice-to-enter-and-stay
condition onto how pleasant the experience was judged and whether the
response was approach or avoidance. It says nothing about how long
anyone thought the wait took. And it's worth naming a distinction the
study's own choice variable raises directly: whether a choice actually
existed (actual control) is not the same as whether the person felt
they had one (perceived control) — the two can diverge, and this
course's own experiment, below, has to keep them apart rather than
treat "control" as one undifferentiated thing.

## Designing the manipulation

Any experiment on control has to resist two easy mistakes. The first is
turning it into a Week 4 experiment by mistake: if a design varies what
information a person is given and then measures "control," it has
tested information, not control, however the write-up labels it. The
second is treating crowding, from this week's own source, as if it were
the thing to manipulate — density is Week 6's mechanism, present here
only as the setting of the source being read, not as this week's
teaching variable.

A course-designed way to avoid both: hold the wait-time and status
information identical across two conditions, and vary only whether a
genuine, no-penalty choice to continue or leave is available. In one
condition, a person must remain until the process finishes; in the
other, they're told plainly they may continue or leave at any point,
with nothing else about the wait changed. One honest complication is
worth stating directly rather than glossing over: telling someone a
choice exists is itself a piece of information — what's held constant
is the wait-time and status information, while the independent variable
is the availability of a genuine choice, not the presence of information
as such. This is a course-designed simulation, not a description of Hui
and Bateson's own procedure, and it tests whatever can actually be
observed — a self-reported sense of control, or whether someone given
the option actually leaves — not judged duration, unless a specific
design deliberately says otherwise.

## Why this matters

Every mechanism from Week 1 to Week 9 was something done *to* or
*communicated to* the person waiting. This is the one about what they
can do about it — the last individually taught mechanism, and the first
one this course teaches through a deliberate manipulation rather than
observation alone. The Waiting Experiment, due at the end of this week,
asks you to design and run exactly one such manipulation, on control by
default or on a mechanism a genuine Case Study gap or a named source
limitation points you toward instead — this lecture isn't asking every
student to test control, only teaching what a control-specific test
looks like when that's the right one to run. And the discipline this
week asks for — separating what's evidenced from what's interpreted,
holding "directly evidenced" apart from "caused" — is exactly what the
Waiting Audit will ask of every one of the ten mechanisms in Week 12,
where a mechanism's classification and the strength of its evidence are
kept as two separate questions, not one.
