Imagine a checkout screen offering two options: free delivery in seven
days, or a few dollars more for delivery tomorrow. This is a hypothetical,
not a documented case — but it's a familiar enough shape to think with.
Nobody here is being served ahead of anyone else. There's no shared queue
at all. The two options simply differ in what you get for what you're
willing to wait.

## Three things this course keeps apart

Week 8 asked what happens when a system deliberately treats classes
differently *within the same queue* — priority as a redistribution of
position. This week is not that question, and it splits into two further
questions that also aren't each other:

1. **Implicit value.** Sometimes a wait is tolerated simply because what's
   coming is worth it. Nobody designed a trade; there's no offer, no
   incentive, no alternative option. The value was just already there in
   what you're waiting for.
2. **Explicit exchange.** Sometimes a system deliberately builds a trade on
   purpose — offering you something (money saved, more flexibility, more
   certainty) in return for a longer or shorter wait. This is a designed
   feature of the system, not a fact about the thing you're waiting for.

Keep these two, plus Week 8's priority redistribution, as three separate
mechanisms. A "pay more, wait less" option can look like all three at once
if you're not careful — this week's job is learning to tell them apart.

## A practitioner's proposition about implicit value

Maister proposed that the more valuable a service is to the person waiting
for it, the more willing they may be to tolerate waiting for it. Treat this
exactly as this course has treated Maister's other propositions before now
(Weeks 2 and 8): a named, long-standing idea from service-management
practice, not a tested finding. Nobody in this course's source list has
measured it. It explains nothing on its own — it names a plausible
tendency worth watching for, and nothing more.

## A measured instance of an explicit exchange

Dietl, Voigt and Kuhn studied one specific version of an explicit trade:
whether offering people an incentive changes their stated willingness to
accept a longer delivery wait when shopping online for fashion, shoes, or
accessories ([Dietl, Voigt & Kuhn,
2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2024.104280)). In a within-subjects,
repeated-measures online survey of 323 participants in a German-speaking
context, they tested several incentives against a longer delivery
scenario. Among their findings: **shipping-cost savings significantly
increased stated willingness to accept a longer delivery wait in the
study.**

Read that claim at exactly the scope it earns. This is one bounded retail
sector — online fashion, shoes, and accessories — with a non-probabilistic
recruited sample, specific delivery-time and incentive levels chosen by the
researchers, and a measure of *stated* willingness rather than *observed*
purchasing or waiting behaviour. It is not a finding that people are
willing to pay more for shorter waits — it's closer to the opposite:
people reported more willingness to accept a *longer* wait when offered a
saving in return. Nothing here generalises to other sectors, to physical
queues, or to waiting in general.

## What this doesn't establish

Dietl et al.'s result doesn't tell you what would happen at a supermarket
checkout, a transit stop, or any queue this course has already studied —
it's a single study of one incentive type in one online retail context,
measuring what people say they'd accept, not what they actually do.
Maister's proposition remains exactly as untested here as it was in Weeks
2 and 8. Neither source licenses a claim about waiting in general, and
neither should be read as proof of the other — one names an unmeasured
tendency about value inherent in an outcome; the other measures a stated
preference under one specific, deliberately designed incentive.

## Why this matters

Waiting is an experience, not merely a duration — and this week adds one
more way that experience can be shaped without anyone's queue position
changing at all: what a wait is exchanged for, whether that exchange was
ever designed or simply already there in what's being waited for. This
closes the block Weeks 5 to 9 have been building, each asking a different
question about how a waiting system is built rather than just how it's
experienced by one person in it: interfaces, environment, queue structure,
priority, and now value. The Queue Case Study, due at the end of this
week, asks you to hold several of these mechanisms together in one real
system — which means telling them apart correctly matters more now than
it ever has.

## What you're doing this week

The session asks you to read one real system for exactly this: does it
change what you get, or does it change who's served first? The two aren't
always distinguishable from the outside, and saying so honestly is itself
part of the answer.
