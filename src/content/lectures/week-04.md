Imagine two queues at the same passport office, same posted average wait,
same kind of morning. One has a screen: "You are number 14. Now serving
number 9." The other has a numbered ticket and nothing else — no screen, no
update, no one telling you anything until your number is finally called.
Nobody in either queue was told a different story about how long this
would take before they joined it. The difference is entirely in what
happens after they're already standing there.

## This week's claim, plainly

*Information received during a wait can affect how the wait is evaluated,
without necessarily producing a corresponding change in judged duration.*
That's the whole claim. It doesn't say information makes a wait feel
shorter, and it doesn't say it always makes a wait feel better — only that
evaluation and judged duration are two different things a wait produces,
and information is a mechanism that can move one without moving the other.

## Redrawing Week 3's line

Week 3 was careful about this and it stays true here: expectation is what
you bring *into* a wait — set before it began. Information is what you
receive *while the wait is already underway* — a countdown, a queue
position, an announcement, someone saying "about five more minutes." Your
log has kept these apart since Week 1: "Expected wait" is written once,
before the clock starts; "Available information" can change, or arrive, at
any point after. A wait can have a confident expectation and zero
information (you knew it would be ten minutes and heard nothing further),
or no expectation at all and a screen counting down the second you sit
down. This week stays entirely on the information side of that line.

## What Hui and Tse actually did

Hui and Tse studied what happens when people are given information about a
wait once it has started, rather than left without it ([Hui & Tse,
1996](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299606000206)). Their measures included
how acceptable the wait felt, the affective response to it, evaluation of
the service afterwards, and a measure of perceived waiting duration
specific to their study's design. Read that last one carefully: their
perceived-waiting-duration measure is not the same instrument as this
course's "Judged duration" field. Theirs was collected under a controlled
experimental setup with a specific scenario and manipulation; yours is a
personal, free-form estimate written in your own words. A finding about
their measure is not automatically a finding about yours, and this week
doesn't assume it is.

What their study licenses is narrower and more useful: information
delivered during a wait can shift acceptability, affect, and evaluation of
the service. It does not show that information makes a wait feel shorter —
that's a specific, further claim about duration judgement that would need
its own evidence, not a paraphrase of what Hui and Tse actually measured.

## Why this isn't a repeat of Week 3

Week 3 introduced Pruyn and Smidts' acceptable waiting time: a tolerance
threshold, separate from what you'd predicted, against which a wait gets
evaluated. Crossing that threshold, in their data, provoked a
disproportionately strong negative reaction. That's a finding about a
reference standard you carry *into* a wait — a tolerance rather than a
prediction, but still something already fixed before the wait starts.
Information is a different kind of mechanism entirely: it isn't a standard
you bring with you, it's something a system hands you only after the wait
is already underway. Week 4 doesn't extend Week 3's reference-standard
story onto a new source — it introduces a second, independent lever, one
that only exists once the wait has already begun.

## What this doesn't establish

This doesn't establish that any information helps, that more information
is always better information, or what happens when the information turns
out to be wrong — a countdown that stalls, a queue position that stops
moving. Hui and Tse's study doesn't speak to inaccurate information at all;
that's a real question this week's source simply doesn't answer, not one
you should assume an answer to either way.

## Why this matters

This closes the block Weeks 1 to 4 have been building: Week 1 measured a
wait, Weeks 2 to 4 each took the same log and asked what explains a
difference between two entries — attention, then expectation, then
information. Waiting Field Notes, worth 15% of the course and due at the
end of this week, asks you to submit that log along with your own
interpretation of what it shows across all three lenses at once, not just
whichever one a given entry happens to fit best. From Week 5, the course
stops reading your personal log and starts examining how waits get built —
starting with the most literal piece of information design there is, the
progress bar.

## What you're doing this week

The session sends you back to the log a third time, this time sorting
entries by what "Available information" actually contained, and asking
whether entries that are similar in clock duration *and* in judged duration
still land differently in Outcome/notes depending on whether information
was available — which is the specific comparison this week's claim is
built on.
