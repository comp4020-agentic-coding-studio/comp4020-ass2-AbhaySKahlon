A hypothetical café — not a system anyone in this course has actually
observed — runs a standard queue and, alongside it, a $2 "priority pickup"
option. Two mechanisms are visible immediately: priority (someone is served
sooner) and value (money is exchanged for that). Week 9 already taught you
to tell those two apart rather than filing the feature under whichever
label is convenient. What Week 9 never asked is whether the two are
*connected* — whether the $2 is what actually grants the faster service,
or whether they merely happen to sit in the same system without one
implementing the other. That's a third, separate claim, and this week
teaches how to make it honestly.

## This week's claim, plainly

*A real system rarely runs one mechanism at a time — mechanisms may depend
on each other, interact, or remain independent, and saying which is true
for a specific pair is a claim that needs its own evidence tier, exactly
like any other claim this course has asked you to make honestly.*

This is the last lecture of the course, and it closes two gaps rather than
opening a new mechanism. Value remains this week's required tag — the
schema needs one, and Week 9 is where this course first taught you to test
whether two mechanisms overlap, so that's the precedent this week
generalises. This is not a second value lesson.

## Where the toolkit still has a hole

By the end of Week 11, you hold nine mechanisms — five carried forward and
re-tiered from your Queue Case Study, four built from scratch — each with a
status and an evidence tier, on the same system revisited across Weeks 9,
11, and 12. Two things are still missing, and neither is "add another
mechanism":

1. **Control has never been classified in this system at all.** Your Week
   10 experiment tested control somewhere — in a simulation, a prototype, or
   on yourself — but that is not the same claim as "this audited system
   contains a control mechanism."
2. **No claim has ever been made about how two mechanisms in this system
   relate to each other.** Every finding so far has been about one
   mechanism in isolation.

## The tenth row: control, classified from this system's own evidence

Control gets exactly the same two-axis treatment as every other mechanism,
built fresh from what your audited system actually shows:

- **Mechanism status** — present / absent / ambiguous / cannot determine.
  Is there a genuine, no-penalty choice to continue or leave visible in
  this system — a way out, a pause, an opt-out — or is there evidence that
  there isn't one?
- **Evidence tier** — directly evidenced / plausible interpretation /
  undetermined, for whatever specific claim you make about it.

**Your Week 10 experiment can provide additional evidence about a
control-related claim, but it does not by itself establish that the
audited system contains a control mechanism. The audited system must still
be classified from its own accessible evidence.** If your experiment tested
a simulation, a prototype, or a different context entirely, that result may
be worth citing as additional evidence where it's genuinely relevant to
this specific system — and just as often, it isn't relevant at all, and the
honest thing to write is that the control row is classified from the
system's own evidence alone, with the experiment noted separately as not
transferable here. A system with no visible way to leave, pause, or opt out
is legitimately **absent**, directly evidenced by that absence, regardless
of what your own experiment found somewhere else.

## The relationship claim: a third question, not a merge of the first two

Two mechanisms can each be individually well-evidenced as present without
that telling you anything about whether they're connected. The connection
itself is a separate claim, answered with one of three outcomes:

- **Interacting** — evidence indicates that one mechanism's operation
  depends on, implements, modifies, or is linked to the other.
- **Independent** — available evidence supports that the mechanisms operate
  separately in this system.
- **Cannot determine** — available evidence is insufficient to establish
  whether they interact or operate independently.

**Two mechanisms merely coexisting in the same system does not establish
that they are independent.** Independence is itself a claim that needs
evidence — a system can have both value and priority present, with no
evidence either way about whether they're connected, in which case "cannot
determine" is the honest relationship finding, not "independent" by
default.

Every relationship claim gets its own evidence tier — directly evidenced,
plausible interpretation, or undetermined — separate from the tiers already
assigned to the two mechanisms individually. And one boundary matters as
much as the tier itself: **a relationship claim is about how two mechanisms
operate in this particular system, not about whether those mechanisms
generally interact across waiting systems.** Finding that value implements
priority at one café says nothing about whether it does anywhere else — the
claim doesn't survive being carried out of the one system it was made
about.

You are not required to consider every possible pair. With ten mechanisms
there are up to forty-five; testing all of them would manufacture work, not
insight. Identify only the pairs where there's a plausible,
evidence-grounded reason to think a connection might exist, and leave the
rest alone. A short relationship log, or an empty one, is a complete and
correct result for a system whose mechanisms genuinely don't show any
evidenced connection to each other.

## The café, worked in full

- **Priority: present.** A stated faster lane exists.
- **Value: present.** A stated $2 price exists.
- **Relationship claim: "the payment buys the priority."** If the system's
  own published terms explicitly state that the $2 option grants faster
  service, this is **interacting**, tiered **directly evidenced** — there's
  no daylight between the two features to interpret, the terms say it
  outright.
- **Claim: "standard-queue customers therefore feel unfairly treated."**
  This is **undetermined**. Nothing about a stated price-for-priority
  relationship tells you how anyone waiting in the standard queue actually
  felt about it — that would require asking them, which this kind of
  observation doesn't permit.

## What this isn't

A write-up that says "this system has value, priority, information, and
control, and together they make the wait feel modern and controlled, which
is why customers keep coming back" fails on every count this course has
spent twelve weeks building against: four mechanisms bundled into one
undifferentiated verdict, a claim about how the wait *feels* with no
evidence for anyone's experience, a claim about *behaviour* (repeat
custom) with no evidence for it, and not one relationship among the four
actually stated or evidenced — "together" is doing all the work a specific,
tiered relationship claim should be doing instead. It's the same move
Maister's own practitioner propositions risk when read uncritically: several
genuinely different mechanisms folded into one intuitive "this is why
waiting here feels fine" story. Useful as a prompt for where to look, as
this course has said of Maister since Week 2 — never a substitute for
naming which specific mechanisms are doing what, and how strongly you can
actually show it.

## Why this matters

Waiting is an experience, not merely a duration — the claim Week 1 made
testable by splitting what a clock shows from what only the waiting
person's own judgement could report. Twelve weeks and ten mechanisms later,
that same split holds at the scale of a whole system: what's directly
observable — a rule, a price, a published term, a feature's presence — and
what's only ever a plausible or undetermined claim about anyone's actual
experience of it. The Waiting Audit does not prove that thesis; nothing a
single system observed a few times over one course could prove it. What
twelve weeks buys you is the discipline to say, for any claim about this
one system, exactly which of those two kinds of thing you're actually
saying — and to say "cannot determine" or "undetermined" as a complete,
honest answer wherever that's what the evidence supports, rather than
rounding up to sound more finished.
