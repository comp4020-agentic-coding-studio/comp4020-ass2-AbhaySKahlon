Two people finish the same eleven-minute wait for a doctor's appointment.
One sat in a room with old chairs, a bare noticeboard, and nothing to
look at. The other sat in a room with better light, something on the
walls, somewhere comfortable to sit. Ask both how long it felt, and you
might get the same answer. Ask both how they feel about the visit, and
you might not.

## This week's claim, plainly

*A waiting environment can shape how a wait is evaluated through affect,
without necessarily changing the perceived length of the wait.* Pruyn
and Smidts, whose work on acceptable waiting time you've already met
twice, found this pattern for perceived waiting-room attractiveness in
their service-waiting study: attractiveness affected satisfaction mainly
through affect and mood, while objective waiting time affected
satisfaction mainly through a separate, cognitive route. Two different
routes, doing different work, on the same wait.

## Not another representation question

Week 5 asked what a digital representation's changing *behaviour*
represented about an unfolding wait — a bar that climbed, paused,
accelerated, making a directional claim about how much was left. A room
makes no such claim. It doesn't climb, it doesn't pause, it isn't
"about" the remaining wait at all. So this week isn't asking what the
room communicates, in the sense Week 5 asked what the bar communicated —
it's asking something narrower and different: what the environment
*does* to the evaluation of the wait, what it makes salient while
someone sits in it, and how environmental conditions relate to affect
rather than to any claim about progress. If this week just swapped
"room" for "screen" and kept asking the same representational question,
it would mean the substrate change hadn't actually changed anything
worth teaching.

## What Pruyn and Smidts actually measured

Their study measured the effects of objective waiting time and two
specific elements of the waiting environment — the attractiveness of
the waiting room, and the presence of a television as an explicit
distractor — on satisfaction with the service, in a real waiting-room
setting, with perceived waiting time, acceptable waiting time, and
cognitive/affective appraisal tracked as the variables in between
([Pruyn & Smidts, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-8116(98)00008-1)).
Two findings matter here that neither Week 3 nor Week 4 used:

- **Attractiveness worked through affect.** Objective waiting time
  affected satisfaction mainly through a cognitive route — perceived
  minutes waited, and a long/short judgment of the wait. The
  attractiveness of the room worked through a different route: mainly
  affective, functioning as something closer to a mood inducer than a
  piece of information about the wait.
- **The TV distractor didn't work as predicted.** They included a
  television specifically as an explicit distraction. It did not
  produce the distraction effect they expected — even though people
  were more likely to watch it the longer, and more boring, the wait
  became.

Read both findings at their actual scope: one study, one real
service-waiting context, two specific environmental elements
(attractiveness, a TV). "Attractiveness" is their own construct, defined
for that study — not a checklist of lighting, colour, or seating this
course can hand you, and not a claim that any particular design choice
is doing the work.

## What this doesn't establish

This doesn't establish that attractive waiting rooms are simply
"better," and it doesn't establish that giving people something to
watch or do will automatically improve a wait — the TV finding is direct
evidence against treating that as automatic. Nor does it establish that
environment can never affect how long a wait is perceived to be, in any
wait, ever — only that in this study, attractiveness's effect on
satisfaction ran mainly through affect rather than through the same
cognitive route objective time used. That's a finding about this study's
own variables, not a general law about environment and duration
judgement.

## Why this matters

Five lenses so far — time, attention, expectation, information, and
interface behaviour — have each given us a different way to read a
wait. Environment adds a genuinely different case: a route through
affect and mood that, in this study, operated mainly separately from
perceived waiting duration, and a reminder that a tested environmental
fix can still fail to work as its designer expected. A physical space
also does one thing this week's mechanism doesn't otherwise require: it
puts a wait's other people, and the order they're standing in, in the
same visible space as you — which is where next week's question about a
queue's own rules will actually start to matter.

## What you're doing this week

Keep the wait-observation log's method, extended with one new field for
what you actually noticed about the physical space of an ordinary wait.
This week's log isn't checking what the space implied about how much
longer the wait would take — it's checking whether anything about the
space shows up in your Outcome/notes at all, independently of whether
the wait felt long or short.
