Imagine two checkouts are open. One arrangement: a single line snakes
toward both, and whoever reaches the front takes whichever counter frees
up next. Another arrangement: two separate lines, one per checkout, and
you pick one when you arrive. You're in the second arrangement. The other
line moves faster — not because anyone cut in, just because that
checkout happens to be quicker this time. Someone who arrived after you
gets served first. Nobody broke a rule. It still doesn't feel that way
from where you're standing.

## The idea

In the study we'll look at, both structures used first-come-first-served
ordering within their queues. What differed was the structure — one
shared line versus several separate ones — and structure changes
something the rule alone doesn't: how easy it is to actually check the
rule is being followed. In a single shared line, everyone's position
relative to everyone else is visible the whole time. In separate lines,
all you can see is your own line; whether the whole system is still
running first-come-first-served is something you have to take on trust,
or infer from a small and possibly misleading sample.

This week calls that gap **legibility** — this course's own term, not
one borrowed from a paper, for whether a person waiting can verify the
rule from where they're standing. It's informed by an older observation
from Larson (1987), who argued that first-come-first-served has long
functioned as a kind of social-justice principle for how queues are
designed and defended, and that people's attitudes shift when the order
they observe seems to violate that principle — whether or not it actually
did. Larson's paper is a framework piece, built from observation and
argument rather than an experiment; treat it as the source of that idea,
not as evidence for it.

## What a controlled comparison found

Rafaeli, Barron and Haber tested this in a controlled, computer-simulated
laboratory queue: participants experienced an actual timed wait, played
out on a screen, structured either as one shared queue or as several
separate queues feeding the same set of service points, with arrival
rate, service speed, and everything else held equal ([Rafaeli, Barron &
Haber, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/109467002237492)). The single- versus
multiple-queue structure was the only thing that varied.

Three measures matter here. Participants in the single-queue structure
reported greater predictability than those in the multiple-queue structure;
predictability rose over the course of the single-queue wait but stayed flat
in the multiple-queue condition. Participants in the single-queue structure
also reported higher arousal, while arousal declined over the wait in the
multiple-queue condition. And participants in the multiple-queue structure
reported lower **perceived justice** than participants in the single-queue
structure.

That last finding is more specific than it first sounds, and the specific
version is the one worth keeping. The researchers separated
multiple-queue participants into those who had actually experienced a
first-come-first-served violation (someone arriving later reached the
front first) and those who had not. Even the multiple-queue participants
who experienced no actual violation — who were treated exactly as
first-come-first-served as anyone in the single queue — still reported
lower perceived justice than single-queue participants. Queue structure
alone lowered the reported sense of justice, with no rule ever broken.

## What this doesn't establish

This was one lab simulation, not a real physical queue: participants
watched their progress on a screen and could not leave their assigned
queue once they'd entered it — which means the study says nothing about
queues where switching lines is possible, a common feature of real
supermarket or bank queues. "Perceived justice" here means exactly what
participants reported feeling on a survey, not a verdict on whether the
queue was actually fair. And "legibility" is this course's own reading of
why structure might produce that reported difference — Rafaeli et al.
tested structure and measured its effect on reported perceived justice;
they did not test, or claim to explain, legibility as the mechanism behind
it.

## Why this matters

Week 6 put you in a room with other people, without yet asking about the
order they were moving in. This week asks exactly that: not whether a
space felt fair, but whether the system directing who goes next was
legible to the person inside it. It extends the course thesis into the
social: waiting is an experience, not merely a duration, and Rafaeli et
al. found lower reported perceived justice in the multiple-queue
structure, including among participants who had not experienced an
actual first-come-first-served violation — evidence that this experience
can be shaped by structure even when a rule was never broken.

Once we understand the rule that structures who goes next, what happens
when different people are governed by different rules? That's next week.
