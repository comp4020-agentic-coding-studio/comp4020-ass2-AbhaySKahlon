## The brief

> Design and run one small, ethical, deliberate manipulation of a single
> named waiting mechanism, and report honestly what it does and doesn't
> show.

Everything before this assessment has been observation — of your own
waits, then of a system you didn't build. This is the course's one
intervention: you choose something, change it on purpose, and see what
happens. It is also the smallest-scale piece of work in the course, and
the brief expects you to know that going in — a bounded exercise like
this can suggest something worth reporting; it cannot prove very much on
its own.

## Starting from a gap, not a hunch

Name your mechanism before you touch any data, and say why it's the right
one to test. There are two legitimate starting points:

1. **Week 10's mechanism: control.** This week's teaching is about the
   sense of control a person has over a wait — whether they can choose
   when to join, how it proceeds, or whether to leave it. Most students
   should start here: pick a control-related manipulation (offering a
   choice over queue position or pacing, versus not) and test it directly.
2. **A gap your own Queue Case Study left open**, or **a specific
   limitation of a named course source.** If your Case Study marked a
   mechanism ambiguous or undeterminable from published information, or if
   a source this course has cited (Hui & Tse, Pruyn & Smidts, Dietl, Voigt
   & Kuhn, or any other) explicitly didn't measure something your own
   question needs, you may test that mechanism instead — but you must name
   the exact gap and why it's worth closing.

A submission that tests an arbitrary mechanism with no stated connection
to either path hasn't met the brief, however well the experiment itself is
run.

## Running the experiment

Default to self-observation, a simulation, or a controlled prototype — the
same default the [Experiment ethics policy](/policies/#experiment-ethics)
sets for this assessment and for the Waiting Audit. A live intervention on
a real waiting system is only permitted where every condition in that
policy is met: genuinely low-risk, non-disruptive, privacy-preserving,
authorised wherever authorisation is needed, and never disrupting an
actual clinic, emergency-service, or similarly essential queue.

## What you submit

In order: the protocol, written before you collected any data (mechanism
and gap, manipulation, measure, prediction); what you actually observed;
the result, including a null, mixed, or contrary-to-prediction outcome
reported exactly as it came out; a bounded interpretation of what that
result does and doesn't show; and a limitations section naming the
confounds a small, largely uncontrolled exercise can't rule out.

## What this can't show

A handful of trials, run by one person, is not a controlled study. It
cannot establish strong causal proof, and it cannot be generalised beyond
the specific context you tested it in. If your result doesn't match a
cited study's finding, that's a limitation of your own small exercise —
not evidence that the cited research is wrong.

## Afterwards

The Waiting Audit, due at the end of Week 12, may use this experiment's
result as evidence for one mechanism in the system you audit — but only
where it's actually relevant to that system, only for the one mechanism
you tested, and only as strongly as your own design here actually
supports.
