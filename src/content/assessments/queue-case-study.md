## The brief

> Take everything Weeks 5 to 9 taught about how a waiting system is built —
> its interface, its environment, its queue structure, its priority rules,
> and its value exchanges — and use it to decompose one real system into the
> mechanisms actually operating in it, rather than describing the experience
> of using it as one undifferentiated whole.

Choose one real waiting system and determine, for each of interface,
environment, queue structure, priority redistribution, and value/attribute
exchange, whether it is present, absent, ambiguous, or cannot be determined
from what you can access. Where a mechanism is present, say specifically
what it does, in this course's own vocabulary for that mechanism — not in
the language of a general customer-experience description. Finding a
mechanism genuinely absent is a legitimate, valued result here, not a gap in
your analysis.

## Choosing a system

Any real-world waiting system with sufficient publicly observable or
documentable features to analyse its waiting mechanisms will do: a café or
retail queue with a posted or ticketed order, a delivery or shipping
checkout with more than one speed or cost option, a call centre or online
support queue with a stated priority category, a transit system with
boarding groups, or something similar. You may reuse or extend a system you
already mapped for Week 7, 8, or 9 — deepening one system's analysis across
mechanisms is worth more here than starting over on a new one.

Carrying forward this course's existing observation rules: no medical,
emergency, clinical, or triage setting. Don't identify, name, or photograph
any real person. Observation must be non-interfering, drawn only from
publicly observable or documentable features — signage, published terms,
a website's stated options, your own non-identifying notes on how a queue
actually behaved. Don't interview staff or customers; nothing in this
course's existing rules extends that permission to this assessment.

## The five mechanisms

You don't need five equal-length sections. Structure the analysis however
the system itself makes sense, but address all five mechanisms somewhere,
with an explicit finding for each:

- **Interface (Week 5):** does the system communicate anything about the
  wait itself — a countdown, a position indicator, a percent-done signal —
  and if so, what does it actually show, versus what it merely implies?
- **Environment (Week 6):** does the physical or digital space shape the
  wait on its own, independent of the queue or the interface — seating,
  layout, sound, visual design?
- **Queue structure (Week 7):** what rule governs order, and could you
  actually verify it from where you stood or from what's published?
- **Priority redistribution (Week 8):** does one class of person get served
  sooner than another, on a stated criterion? A separate faster service tier
  is not automatically priority redistribution. Priority requires an
  ordering relationship in which one class is served sooner relative to
  another class in the same service/ordering system. Identify what ordering
  relationship actually exists before applying the Week 8 label — if the
  faster and slower options share no queue or order position, that is not
  priority, whatever else it might be.
- **Value/attribute exchange (Week 9):** does the system trade time against
  money, flexibility, certainty, or another attribute, explicitly? Classify
  this half of the system as value/attribute exchange, priority
  redistribution, both, or cannot determine from published information or
  evidence, using the same test Week 9 taught: does it change what's
  received, or does it change who's served first? Implicit value is a
  practitioner proposition from Maister, not an observable finding
  established by this case study. You may discuss perceived value as a
  possible interpretation only where relevant, but must not present it as an
  observed mechanism or empirical finding about users.

A single feature can implicate more than one mechanism. Say exactly which,
rather than filing it under whichever label is most convenient.

## What you submit

Findings for all five mechanisms, each with its evidence and its
present/absent/ambiguous/cannot-determine call, structured however suits
your system. Close with a required synthesis: which mechanisms interacted
with each other, which were fully independent, where the available evidence
was insufficient to say, and how you kept the classifications distinct
rather than letting them blend into one description of "the experience."

End with a limitations section naming what this analysis can't establish:
the limits of what you could observe or document, the time window you
observed it in, the impossibility of inferring anyone's psychology from
what you saw, the absence of any controlled comparison, and the fact that
one system observed once is not evidence about that type of system in
general.

Course readings — Harrison, Amento, Kuznetsov and Bell; Myers; Pruyn and
Smidts; Larson; Rafaeli, Barron and Haber; Raz, Avi-Itzhak and Levy;
Maister; Dietl, Voigt and Kuhn — are analytical lenses for interpreting what
you observe, not evidence that your chosen system produces the same effects
those studies measured in their own contexts.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, or an equivalent mix of text and
evidence — screenshots and photographs of signage or published terms don't
count toward that limit.

## What you must not claim

Without evidence for it, don't claim what anyone felt, satisfaction,
fairness, efficiency, willingness to pay, why the organisation designed the
system that way, causality, or general customer or user behaviour.
"Cannot determine from available evidence" is a legitimate, successful
finding wherever it's honest — not a failure to find something.

## How this is marked

Marking rewards analytical accuracy, not the number of mechanisms found. A
system that genuinely exhibits only two or three of the five mechanisms can
receive full marks if the analysis correctly establishes the others as
absent or undeterminable, with evidence for that call. A submission that
strains to manufacture a mechanism that isn't there will score worse than
one that states its absence plainly.

## Afterwards

This is the last piece of work before the course turns from analysis to
intervention. The Waiting Experiment and the Waiting Audit both depend on
the same discipline this case study tests: separating what a system does
from how it might feel, and naming plainly what the evidence in front of
you can't establish.
