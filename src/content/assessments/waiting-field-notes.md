## The brief

> Submit the wait-observation log you've kept since Week 1, and show that
> you can read it through all three explanatory lenses this block has
> built — attention, expectation, information — together, not one at a
> time.

This is a personal record of waits you actually experienced, not an
external system you've gone looking for. Nothing here asks you to analyse
how a system is built — that's the Queue Case Study's job later in the
course. This assessment asks something narrower and more basic: can you
read your own log honestly, against the specific distinctions Weeks 1 to 4
gave you, without overclaiming what a handful of personal entries can
show?

## What you submit

The log itself, in full, covering Weeks 1 to 4: every entry's clock
duration, judged duration and whether that judgement was prospective or
retrospective, expected wait, available information, and outcome/notes.
Alongside it, a written interpretation that works through the log using
each week's own distinction:

- **Week 1 — clock vs. judged duration.** Where do the two numbers agree
  or diverge across your entries, and which of those judgements were made
  prospectively versus retrospectively?
- **Week 2 — attention.** Is what occupied you during a wait consistent
  with differences between entries' judged durations? This can only ever
  show consistency or inconsistency with the pattern the research
  describes, not prove it — say so plainly rather than overstating what
  one log can establish.
- **Week 3 — expectation.** How does "Expected wait" line up against
  outcome? Keep this apart from information: expectation is set before a
  wait begins, and Week 4 depends on that line staying clear.
- **Week 4 — information.** How does "Available information" line up
  against outcome, independently of what you expected going in?

Close by naming any entries that don't fit any of these lenses cleanly.
That's a legitimate, useful thing to report, not a gap in your log.

## What you must not claim

Don't claim that your log proves any of the four mechanisms — a personal
log, checked against a handful of entries, can only show whether a pattern
is consistent with what you recorded, never that it caused anything. Don't
generalise your own experience to other people or to waiting in general.
Don't treat a self-reported, unblinded personal record as if it were a
controlled measurement.

## How this is marked

Marking assesses the completeness and fidelity of the four-week record —
whether it reads as an honest, continuous account of real waits, checked
against every week's distinction — not how many entries it contains. A
shorter log that is complete, honest, and carefully read against all four
lenses will score better than a long one that's thin on interpretation or
inconsistent with its own entries.

## Afterwards

From Week 5, the course stops reading your personal log and starts
examining how waits get built. The same discipline this assessment asks
for — separating what your evidence actually shows from what you're
interpreting into it — is exactly what the Queue Case Study needs once
it turns from your own experience to a system you don't control.
