## The brief

> Revisit the system you analysed in the Queue Case Study, and evaluate it
> against the course's complete ten-mechanism taxonomy — separating what
> you can classify as present in the system from how strongly your
> evidence actually supports any claim about it.

This is not a second case study. You are not starting over on a new
system, and you are not re-running the five-mechanism analysis from
scratch. You're extending the same analysis to the five mechanisms your
Case Study didn't cover, and re-examining everything — old and new —
through a stricter question than "is this present": *how strong is the
evidence for what I'm claiming about it?*

## The same system, twice

Use the system you analysed for the Queue Case Study. A different system
is permitted only where the original has become genuinely inaccessible,
unsafe, or no longer observable — and if you make that change, say so
briefly and explain why. Carry your Case Study's five findings (interface,
environment, queue structure, priority, value) forward into this
assessment's structure rather than re-deriving them; your work here is to
extend the analysis to time, attention, expectation, information, and
control, and to apply the evidence-tier layer below across all ten.

## Two questions, kept apart

Every mechanism gets answered twice, and the two answers are not the same
question:

- **Mechanism classification** — present, absent, ambiguous, or cannot
  determine. This asks: *what appears to be in the system?*
- **Evidence tier** — directly evidenced, plausible interpretation, or
  undetermined. This asks: *how strongly can what I have actually support
  this claim?*

**Directly evidenced does not mean caused.** A feature or result can be
directly evidenced without establishing its causal effect on experience.
A countdown timer's presence on a checkout screen is directly evidenced —
you can see it, document it, quote it. Whether that timer actually changes
anyone's judged wait is, without a controlled test, at most a plausible
interpretation. A mechanism classified "present" can sit alongside a claim
tiered no higher than "plausible interpretation" — that's a common,
correct combination here, not a weak submission.

## Where the experiment fits

State plainly whether your Week 10 Waiting Experiment is relevant to the
system you're auditing. If it is: name the exact mechanism it tested and
the conditions it tested under, and tier that specific claim according to
how strong your own experimental design actually was — a thin, mixed, or
uncontrolled result doesn't automatically earn "directly evidenced" just
because you ran an experiment. If it isn't relevant, say so directly
rather than forcing a connection. Either way, your experiment's result
speaks only to the one mechanism it tested — it must never be stretched to
support a claim about any of the other nine.

## What you submit

The ten-mechanism classification, with the five from your Case Study
carried forward and re-tiered rather than redone; an evidence tier for
every claim you make about a mechanism; the experiment cross-reference
above; a whole-system synthesis pulling the ten mechanisms together; and a
limitations section addressing the scope of a ten-mechanism, single-system
audit — what you could and couldn't observe, the time window, the
impossibility of inferring anyone's psychology from what you saw, and the
absence of a controlled comparison beyond whatever your one experiment
actually tested.

## What you must not claim

No redesign, no recommendation, no fairness or efficiency verdict. No
claim about what anyone felt, satisfaction, willingness to pay, or why the
system's owner built it that way, without evidence for it. "Undetermined"
is a legitimate, correct answer wherever it's honest — for a mechanism
classification, or for an evidence tier, or both.

## How this is marked

Marking rewards accuracy and honest tiering across all ten mechanisms, not
the number of mechanisms found present or the number of claims pushed to
"directly evidenced." A system correctly and thoroughly classified, with
most claims honestly sitting at "plausible interpretation," will score
better than one that overreaches its evidence to look more conclusive than
it is.

## Afterwards

This is the course's last piece of work. Waiting is an experience, not
merely a duration — the claim Week 1 made testable rather than merely
plausible. This audit is where that claim gets applied in full, across
every mechanism this course named, on the one real system you've now
examined twice.
