Week 6 put you in a room with other people. This week asks about the
order they're moving in. Instead of another field on the running log,
this week's task is a small case study: one queue, one rule, one honest
comparison.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 7 lecture](/lectures/week-07/) first. Keep its distinction
in mind: a queue's *structure* — how its lines are arranged — isn't the
same thing as its *rule* — what's supposed to decide who goes next. This
week is about whether you could actually verify the rule from where you
stood.

## Finding a queue

Pick one ordinary, real queue you can watch safely and without getting
involved — a café counter, a shop checkout, a bus stop, a campus service
window. Not a medical facility, an emergency service, or anything
sensitive. Observe only: don't join it to test it, don't change your
position, and don't photograph, name, or otherwise identify anyone in it.

## Reading the rule

Answer three questions, in order:

1. **What's the rule?** Stated outright — a sign, a ticket machine, a
   "form one line" instruction — or inferred from watching? Write down
   what you believe it to be, and how you know.
2. **What did you see?** Record at least three consecutive instances of
   who got served or moved forward next.
3. **Do they match?** Compare the sequence to the rule: consistent,
   inconsistent, or too ambiguous to tell.

**A worked example** (a model of the reasoning, not a real observation):

> Queue: a café counter, one line, two baristas. Rule: no sign, but
> everyone forms one line and is called to whichever barista is free —
> inferred first-come-first-served. Observed: three customers served in
> the order they joined. **Comparison:** consistent, as far as I could
> tell — though I could only see the front clearly, so I can't rule out
> something I missed further back. That's a limit on what I checked, not
> evidence the rule held perfectly.

If you find a genuine inconsistency, treat it as a legibility question
first — could you actually see enough to be sure, or is there a plausible
reason you didn't observe (a phone order, someone returning to a held
spot)? Don't call it a broken rule unless you have real grounds to. If you
found nothing inconsistent, or had no way to check, say that plainly —
"I couldn't verify it" is a legitimate finding here, not a failed one.

## Connecting to the lecture

Say whether your case fits the pattern Rafaeli et al. found — that a
queue's *structure*, not just an actual broken rule, can lower reported
perceived justice — or departs from it. One entry can't confirm or refute
that finding; it can only show whether your case is consistent with it.
Any judgement about fairness is your own reading, not a verdict on the
queue.

## In the session

Bring your rule, your observed sequence, and your comparison. We compare
across the room: whose rule was stated versus inferred, whose sequence
matched, and what "too ambiguous to tell" looked like for different
people's queues.

## What this can and can't show

One queue, however carefully read, can't establish that any structure or
rule is fair or unfair in general — only whether your case is consistent
with a pattern found in a different, controlled setting. This stays
smaller than the Week 9 Queue Case Study: one queue, one rule, one
observation window, one comparison. Don't intervene, don't manufacture an
inconsistency, and don't identify anyone you watched.

## Afterwards

Keep the log running. Week 8 asks what happens when a queue's rule isn't
the same for everyone in it.
