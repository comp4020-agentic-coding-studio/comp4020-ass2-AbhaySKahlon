Week 7 asked whether you could verify a queue's rule. This week the rule is
already visible and stated outright — the task is to read exactly what it
changes, and to be honest about what it doesn't tell you.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 8 lecture](/lectures/week-08/) first. Keep its distinction in
mind: a priority rule's *criterion* — what earns it — is separate from what
it actually *changes* for the people who don't have it.

## Finding a policy

Find one real, publicly published priority policy — stated outright, in
writing, by a real organisation: a supermarket express-lane sign, an
airline's boarding-priority tiers, a retailer's loyalty checkout tier, a
call centre's stated priority category, or a government service's
priority-processing category. Don't invent one. Don't use a medical,
emergency, clinical, or triage setting.

## Building the priority map

Answer five questions, in order, from the policy's own published wording
wherever you can:

1. **Who is the priority class?** Quote or closely paraphrase how the
   policy itself describes who qualifies.
2. **What exact criterion creates that class?** Something stated and
   checkable — fewer than a stated number of items, existing account,
   membership tier — not your guess at the organisation's reason for it.
3. **What does the rule change about who moves first?** Describe the
   mechanical change to ordering: who now goes ahead of whom, and under
   what circumstance.
4. **Who remains under the ordinary rule?** State plainly what governs
   everyone not in the priority class.
5. **What information would we need to evaluate the resulting
   redistribution?** Name the specific missing information — each class's
   typical service time, class sizes, arrival rates — that a formal
   analysis like Raz et al.'s would require before anyone could say
   whether this redistribution is even favourable by that measure. This is
   a request to name what's missing, not to estimate it.

**Do not calculate anything.** No fairness score, no RAQFM, no verdict on
whether the policy is mathematically fair — that data doesn't exist for a
publicly posted policy, and this exercise isn't asking you to invent it.

## The closing distinction

End your entry with two separately labelled statements:

- **What this policy tells us** — items 1–4, read directly off the
  published policy.
- **What we'd need more information to establish** — item 5, plus anything
  about how people in either class actually experience the wait, which no
  published policy states and this exercise cannot determine.

Keep these visibly separate. Don't let one bleed into the other.

## What's out of bounds

Don't decide whether the policy is fair. Don't redesign it. Don't render a
moral verdict. Don't claim the policy is or isn't "mathematically fair" or
"biased" — you don't have the data, and neither does anyone reading a public
sign.

## In the session

Bring your priority map. We compare across the room: whose criterion was
crisp and checkable versus vague, whose "ordinary rule" was itself hard to
state, and where everyone's missing-information list converged.

## What this can and can't show

One policy's public wording can tell you what a system says it's doing and
roughly what it mechanically changes. It cannot tell you whether that change
is fair, efficient, or resented — those need data or evidence this exercise
doesn't have. That gap is the point, not a flaw in the exercise.

## Afterwards

Keep the log running. Week 9 asks what the whole waiting system is actually
exchanging for whatever waiting it reduces.
