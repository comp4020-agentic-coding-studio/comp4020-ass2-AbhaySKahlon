Your log now has entries with real disagreements in judged duration, and
this session is where you stop just recording that and start asking why —
starting with a field you already filled in every time: what you were
attending to.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 2 lecture](/lectures/week-02/) first. It explains why
"occupied time feels shorter" isn't the whole story, and why the direction
of attention's effect depends on the same prospective/retrospective
distinction Week 1 introduced. Nothing else to prepare — bring your log.

## Rereading the log

Pick at least three of your existing entries. For each one, answer from what
you already wrote down, not from memory of the wait itself:

- What occupied your attention for most of the wait — the "Attending to"
  field?
- How much attention did that demand? Reading a countdown is a different
  load from staring at nothing, and both are different again from actively
  working a phone.
- Were you deliberately monitoring how much time had passed, or not
  thinking about the clock at all?

Then compare pairs. Look for two entries with a similar clock duration but a
noticeably different judged duration, and ask whether a difference in
attention is a plausible part of the explanation — not the only possible
one, just a candidate that fits.

**A worked comparison** (a model of the reasoning, not a real pair of
entries):

> Entry A: waiting for a lift, clock duration two minutes, judged duration
> "about two minutes," prospective — decided beforehand to log it —
> attending to: watching the floor indicator count up. Entry B: waiting for
> a lift, clock duration two minutes, judged duration "felt like five,"
> retrospective — only decided to log it once the doors opened and someone
> nearby complained — attending to: nothing in particular, on a phone.
> Attention looks like part of the story: A's attention was anchored to a
> visible, monitored process, B's wasn't. But A and B also differ in
> paradigm, and Week 1 already showed paradigm alone can move a judged
> duration. One pair of entries can't tell you which difference is doing the
> work, or whether both are — it can only tell you that a difference in
> attention is consistent with what you're seeing here.

## Logging one more entry, deliberately

Add one new entry, in the exact log format from Week 1, from an ordinary
wait chosen for its attentional condition rather than its convenience — if
your existing entries lean occupied, look for an unoccupied one this week,
or the other way round. Keep it low-risk and ordinary: your own waits, not
anyone else's, and nothing that requires manipulating another person or a
public system to arrange.

## In the session

Bring your reread entries and the new one. We compare across the room: who
found attention a plausible explanation for a difference in their log, who
found it didn't fit, who found their log genuinely couldn't tell either way.
The point of comparing is to see how differently the same question lands on
different logs, not to agree on a single verdict.

## What this can and can't show

A handful of personal entries can't isolate attention from everything else
that might have differed between two waits at the same time — the paradigm,
what you expected going in, what you were told about the wait's length.
Finding that attention fits one pair of entries is a reason to keep asking
the question, not a finding you can generalise from. That separation gets
sharper over the next two weeks, once expectation and information are also
on the table as candidates.

## Afterwards

Keep the log running. Week 3 asks a different question of the same entries —
what you expected the wait to be before it started — so don't treat any
entry as fully explained by attention alone; you'll be rereading it again.
