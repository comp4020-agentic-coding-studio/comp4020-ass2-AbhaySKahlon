Week 8 asked you to map a policy that gives one class of people priority.
This week's system may or may not involve priority at all — the task is to
work out which mechanism you're actually looking at before you say
anything else about it.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 9 lecture](/lectures/week-09/) first. Keep its three-way
distinction in mind: the implicit value of an outcome, an explicit trade a
system builds on purpose, and priority redistribution (Week 8) are not the
same mechanism, and a single system can look like more than one of them at
once.

## Finding a system

Find one real, publicly published system offering at least two options
that differ in wait time — delivery-speed tiers at checkout, a paid
queue-skip pass, standard versus expedited processing, or similar. Use
only what the system actually publishes: a stated price, a stated time,
a stated condition.

## Building the map

Answer these questions, in order, using only the published information:

1. **What are the stated options?** Record them as published — time,
   cost, and any other terms named.
2. **What exactly changes between them?** Time only, or also money,
   flexibility, certainty, or the outcome/service itself.
3. **Is there a shared queue or order position?** Does choosing the faster
   or slower option move you ahead of or behind other people in the same
   line, or are the options entirely separate processes with no shared
   position at all?
4. **Classify the system.** Answer this question directly: *does this
   system change the value or attributes received, or does it change who
   is served first?* Choose one — **value/attribute exchange**, **priority
   redistribution**, **both**, or **cannot determine from published
   information** — and justify your choice using only what you found in
   steps 1–3. If the published information doesn't let you tell, say so;
   that's a legitimate answer here, not a failed one.
5. **What information would we need to judge whether the trade is
   worthwhile?** Name what's missing — how much people actually value the
   time saved or spent, how often each option is chosen, anything else a
   published policy doesn't tell you. Don't estimate it.

## What's out of bounds

Don't offer a fairness verdict, a pricing recommendation, or a redesign.
Don't claim any finding about willingness-to-pay beyond what this course's
sources actually measured in their own bounded contexts — Dietl, Voigt and
Kuhn's finding was about willingness to accept a *longer* wait for a
shipping-cost saving in one online retail context, not a general claim
about paying for speed, and it isn't yours to extend to your own example.

## In the session

Bring your map. We compare across the room: whose system was clearly one
mechanism, whose looked like both at once, and whose published information
simply wasn't enough to classify.

## What this can and can't show

Reading a system's published terms can tell you what it offers and
roughly how it's structured. It cannot tell you whether the trade is
actually worth it to the people choosing it, or what they'd say if asked —
that needs evidence this exercise doesn't have.
