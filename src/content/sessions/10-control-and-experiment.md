Week 10's lecture named two easy mistakes: turning a control experiment
into an information experiment by accident, and using crowding — this
week's own source's setting — as if it were the thing to manipulate. This
session exists to catch both, in your own design, before the assessment
does.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 10 lecture](/lectures/week-10/) first. Bring a draft
protocol, even a rough one: your starting point, what you'd manipulate,
what you'd hold constant, what you'd measure, and your prediction.

## Choosing a starting point

Two legitimate ways in, matching the Waiting Experiment brief:

1. **Week 10's mechanism: control.** Vary the availability of a genuine,
   no-penalty choice to continue or leave a wait, while holding wait-time
   and status information constant across conditions.
2. **A named gap.** A mechanism your Queue Case Study marked ambiguous or
   undeterminable, or a specific, statable limitation of a course source
   — named exactly, not gestured at.

Nobody is required to test control. What everyone is required to do is
name, precisely, why their chosen mechanism is the right one to test.

## The five questions

Bring your protocol to the room. For every design in the session,
including your own, answer these in order:

1. **Is there exactly one manipulated variable?** If two things differ
   between conditions, the design tests neither cleanly.
2. **If you're testing control, is control actually what's varying?** A
   design that changes what information a participant receives, and then
   measures "control," has tested information — however it's labelled.
3. **Are the other mechanisms held constant, as far as reasonably
   possible?** Name what you're holding fixed — the wait-time and status
   information, the environment, the queue structure, the priority
   class, the value on offer — not just what you're varying.
4. **Does your measure actually match your manipulation?** A design that
   varies choice but measures judged duration is measuring the wrong
   thing unless duration is specifically what you predicted would move.
5. **Does the design satisfy the [experiment-ethics
   policy](/policies/#experiment-ethics)?** Self-observation, a
   simulation, or a controlled prototype by default. No live intervention
   on a real, non-consenting person or a real public queue.

A design that fails any of these gets revised in the room, not carried
forward as-is.

## A worked example to test the questions against

A controlled prototype, run with a few consenting participants: a mock
waiting screen shows identical wait-time and status information in both
conditions. In condition A, participants are told they must wait until
the process finishes. In condition B, participants are told, up front,
they may continue or leave at any point with no penalty. This is a
course-designed simulation, not Hui and Bateson's own procedure, and it
doesn't test judged duration — the measure is a short self-report of
perceived control, and/or whether a participant given the option actually
leaves. Note honestly, in your own write-up if you use a design like
this, that telling someone a choice exists is itself information; what's
held constant is the wait-time and status information, and the variable
under test is the availability of the choice itself.

## What's out of bounds

No design that requires a live intervention on a real queue or on
non-consenting people. No design that quietly manipulates crowding and
calls it control. No prediction written or revised after seeing a
result.

## What this can and can't show

A protocol checked in this room is a better-designed protocol — it isn't
a run experiment. Nothing here tells you what your actual result will
be, and nothing here turns a small, largely uncontrolled exercise into a
controlled study.

## Afterwards

Take your checked protocol into the Waiting Experiment, due at the end
of this week: prediction, protocol, manipulation, measure, observation,
bounded interpretation, limitations, in that order. Whatever you find,
the Waiting Audit in Week 12 will ask you to state whether that result is
relevant to the system you're auditing, name exactly what it tested, and
tier it honestly — a mechanism's classification and the strength of its
evidence are two separate questions there, and keeping them separate
starts here.
