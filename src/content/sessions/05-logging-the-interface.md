Four weeks of this log have been about waits that happened to you,
reread through whatever mechanism that week introduced. This week
extends the same instrument — it doesn't replace it — with one new field,
pointed at a wait a system built on purpose.

## Before the session

Read the [Week 5 lecture](/lectures/week-05/) first. It draws the line
this session works from: Week 4 asked whether information was available
during a wait at all; this week asks what the specific behaviour of a
progress or status representation communicated about the process as it
unfolded. Keep those two questions apart, and keep in mind that Harrison
et al.'s findings were measured under a short, fixed, controlled
duration — not a claim about what you should expect to feel during a real
multi-minute wait.

## Finding a genuine entry

Over the week, find one ordinary digital wait with a visible progress or
status representation: a percentage bar, a stepped install sequence, a
download counter, a countdown, or an indeterminate spinner that makes no
positional claim at all. An indeterminate spinner counts — it's a useful
boundary case, since it represents a wait's state by representing almost
nothing about it. Don't manufacture a wait for this — an app update you
were already going to run, a page you were already going to load, a file
you were already going to send. Log it in the existing format from Week 1
(Context, Expected wait, Clock duration, Judged duration,
Prospective/retrospective, Attending to, Available information,
Outcome/notes), and add one new, clearly labelled field:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Interface representation | What the progress or status display actually did over the course of the wait — steady and linear, paused partway through, accelerating near the end, jumping, resetting, or indeterminate (giving no numeric or positional claim at all) |

Describe the behaviour, not just its presence. "There was a progress bar"
answers Week 4's question, not this week's.

## Writing the two-part comparison

Once you have the entry, write a short comparison in two explicit parts:

1. **What the behaviour communicated, partway through** — based on what
   the representation was doing partway through, what did its behaviour
   seem to communicate about the rest of the wait? (Nearly done, stalled,
   no basis for any read at all.)
2. **What actually happened** — did the wait's ending match that, or
   contradict it (a bar that looked nearly finished, then stalled; a
   spinner that offered no basis for any read, and simply ended)?

**A worked comparison** (a model of the reasoning, not a real
observation):

> Context: installing a software update, phone in hand, nothing else to
> do. Expected wait: "a couple of minutes, it usually says." Clock
> duration: 3 minutes 40 seconds. Judged duration: "felt like it dragged
> near the end." Prospective/retrospective: prospective — I decided to
> log this one before starting the update. Attending to: the screen,
> mostly. Available information: a percentage counter. Outcome/notes:
> relieved when it finally moved past 90%. Interface representation:
> climbed steadily to about 90% within the first minute, then sat at
> 90–94% for over two minutes before jumping to 100% in under a second.
> **Communicated partway through:** at 90% within a minute, the bar's
> behaviour up to that point suggested maybe fifteen more seconds.
> **What actually happened:** contradicted it — the stall near the end
> ran far longer than the rest of the wait combined, then resolved all at
> once.

## Connecting to the lecture, without overclaiming

Tie your comparison to at least one specific point from this week's
lecture: Harrison et al.'s finding that a pause near the end of a
progress bar's run was reliably perceived as slower in their controlled,
fixed-duration comparison, or Myers's finding that a formal experiment
found a significant preference for having an indicator present at all.
Say which point your entry is consistent with, or which one it doesn't
seem to fit — one entry can show your observation lines up with, or
departs from, a documented pattern; it can't confirm or refute the
finding itself.

## In the session

Bring your entry and your two-part comparison. We compare across the
room: whose interfaces climbed steadily and stayed consistent with their
own behaviour, whose stalled or jumped against what they seemed to be
doing, and what that did to Outcome/notes regardless of clock duration.

## What this can and can't show

One logged interface, however carefully described, can't establish that
pauses or acceleration have any particular effect on you specifically —
it can only tell you whether your own experience of one representation is
consistent with what Harrison et al. found in a completely different,
controlled setting. Don't build, delay, or otherwise interfere with an
interface to manufacture a more interesting entry; log what you actually
encountered. That kind of deliberate intervention belongs to Week 10, not
here.

## Afterwards

Keep the log running in this new direction. Week 6 asks the same
underlying question of a physical room instead of a screen — what a
waiting environment's own design communicates about how a wait will go —
so the next entry you're building toward doesn't need a screen at all.
