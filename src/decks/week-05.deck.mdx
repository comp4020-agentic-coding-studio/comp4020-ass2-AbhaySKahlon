## A caution before the evidence

"Representation," "shape," "trajectory," "implied completion" — this
course's own vocabulary.

Not the research literature's. The measured part is narrower: perceived
speed, preference, under a fixed duration.

## Harrison, Amento, Kuznetsov & Bell (2007)
### "Rethinking the Progress Bar" — UIST'07

Nine synthetic progress-bar behaviours. 22 participants. Paired
comparisons.

---

## The task

Two bars, side by side. Which one *seemed* to finish faster?

Every bar — **every trial** — ran for exactly **5.5 seconds**, fixed.

```notes
Emphasise out loud: identical, fixed, very short duration. This is the
scope limit the rest of the deck keeps returning to. Not expectation,
not a longer real wait, not the course's own judged-duration field.
```

---

## What they found

- A pause **near the end** → perceived as slower
- Acceleration **near the end** → perceived as faster, preferred
- Whichever bar was seen **first** → mildly preferred, regardless of
  behaviour

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## What this is *not*

Not a study of real, minutes-long waits.

Not a measure of what participants expected.

Not this course's free-form "Judged duration" field.

Not "progress bars make waits feel shorter," universally.

---

## Myers (1985) — a formal experiment

Percent-done indicators:

- give information about progress and completion
- help estimate how much is done, or roughly when a task will finish
- a formal experiment found a **significant preference** for systems
  with an indicator over systems without one

---

## Two papers, two different questions

| Source | Question | Maps onto |
|---|---|---|
| Myers | What changes when a progress indicator is **present**? | Connects back to Week 4's disclosure question |
| Harrison et al. | What changes when the progress **behaviour itself** changes? | Week 5's new question |

"Shape / trajectory / representation" is our analytical vocabulary,
layered on top of these two findings — not a term either paper used.

---

## What none of this licenses

- That an accelerating bar is always the better design
- That a fast finish always reassures — a payment, a medical result?
- That any of this generalises past a short, fixed, controlled
  comparison
